1. Research Question

Does immigration lower wages for native workers? Specifically: what is the direction, magnitude, and distributional incidence of the effect of immigrant labor supply on the wages of native-born workers — distinguishing short-run from long-run, average from sub-group effects, and local from national identification?

---

2. Literature Consensus

There is no clean consensus on direction, but there is a strong consensus on magnitude: effects are small. The pooled estimate (weighted score −0.12, "MIXED," 19 claims, 7 high-confidence) reflects genuine disagreement on sign but convergence on the conclusion that immigration's average wage effect on natives is near zero in the long run and modestly negative in the short run.

The dominant view, supported by the highest-weight methods, has three pillars:

1. Average long-run effect is approximately zero or slightly positive. The structural literature (Ottaviano-Peri-type models accounting for imperfect substitution between natives and immigrants and capital adjustment) finds +0.6% long-run average native wages, −0.4% short-run. A second structural finding reports a positive average effect outright. The meta-analytic conclusion that long-term average impacts are "probably zero at worst" reinforces this — economies absorb labor through capital deepening and immigrant consumption demand.

2. Distributional incidence is real but concentrated. The losers are narrowly defined: native workers without a high-school degree (−0.7% short-run, +0.3% long-run) and, most sharply, prior immigrants (≈ −6%), who are the closest substitutes for new arrivals. The factor-proportions structural work confirms immigration widened educational wage differentials by depressing less-educated native wages.

3. Mechanisms attenuate the negative shock. Task specialization (immigrants → manual jobs, natives → communication-intensive jobs), native internal out-migration (attenuates measured local effects by 40–60%), and innovation/productivity channels (1pp immigrant college-graduate share → 9–18% more patents per capita) all offset downward wage pressure.

Methodological backbone: structural general-equilibrium models, IV (including functional IV and tightness instruments), and OLS spatial-correlation studies, supplemented by natural experiments and meta-analyses.

---

3. Where the Literature Disagrees

The disagreement is on sign for low-skilled natives and on the validity of spatial identification — not on average magnitude.

Fault line 1 — Local vs. national identification (the central methodological battle). OLS/natural-experiment studies exploit cross-area variation and typically find small or zero local effects. But the native out-migration finding (40–60% attenuation) implies local estimates are biased toward zero because the labor-supply shock diffuses spatially. Structural and factor-proportions approaches that estimate at the national level recover larger negative effects for substitutable groups. This is the Card (spatial) vs. Borjas (national skill-cell) divide in microcosm.

Fault line 2 — Sign for low-skilled natives. Contested:

  • The structural search model for Italy: low-skilled immigration boosts GDP and high-skilled markets but is inconclusive for low-skilled native employees.
  • One "other"-method US study argues immigration's effect on wage inequality and less-skilled men has been minor.
  • Factor-proportions and natural-experiment work argue it is negative and meaningful for that group.

    • Fault line 3 — Explicitly contested claims. Two claims are flagged as actively contested:

  • The functional IV methodological paper — its estimate is treated as illustration, not a settled magnitude.
  • The "greasing the wheels" efficiency argument (immigration improves labor-market allocation via native mobility) — theoretically attractive but empirically unverified here.

    • Fault line 4 — Skill returns and downgrading. The German evidence (refugees downgrade more than economic immigrants, lower returns to identical skills) reframes the question: immigrant wage losses may absorb shocks that would otherwise hit natives — but this is untested against the wage-competition claims.

    4. What the Data Shows

    The FRED/World Bank series describe the US labor-market environment but cannot directly identify a causal immigration→wage channel (no immigration-share series, no skill-cell wage decomposition). With that caveat:

  • Real wages rose, not fell. Median real weekly earnings (LES1252881600Q) sit at 376 (1982–84 $), near the top of the 329–393 range. Real wages trended up over the sample. Nominal average hourly earnings (CES0500000008) nearly doubled (15.9 → 30.66). This period coincided with sustained immigration — consistent with the "no aggregate wage suppression" view, inconsistent with a strong negative average effect.

  • Low-skill segment is tight, not slack. Unemployment for less-than-HS workers (LNS14032183) is at 3.5%, near its historic low (range 2.0–15.0). Food-services hourly earnings (CES7072200008) rose from 8.26 to 19.0 — the fastest-growing low-wage series shown. This directly challenges the strong-version claim that immigration durably depresses low-skill native wages; the bottom of the distribution tightened and its wages rose.

  • Aggregate slack is low. Global/modeled unemployment at 4.79% (bottom of its 4.79–6.59 range); nonfarm payrolls (PAYEMS) at an all-time high of 159,001k. Labor-force participation at 61.8% is below its 67.3% peak — a structural decline, but driven by aging/demographics rather than wage competition.

    • Verdict from data: The macro time series are consistent with the dominant "small-to-zero average, tight low-wage market" view and inconsistent with the strong negative hypothesis. But these are aggregate trends confounded by the business cycle, monetary policy, and minimum-wage changes — they corroborate the direction of the consensus but cannot isolate the immigration coefficient. The short-run/sub-group negative effects in the structural literature (−0.4% to −0.7%) are too small to be visible in this data resolution.

    6. Research Gaps (The Gap Matrix)

    Method × Geography gaps (explicit):

  • No OLS studies for Canada, Italy, OECD, Germany, wealthy countries, or the US/Canada/W. Europe pool — OLS spatial-correlation designs are nearly US-monopolized.
  • No Difference-in-Differences for the United States or Canada — striking, given DiD is the natural design for discrete immigration shocks (e.g., visa-lottery, refugee-resettlement, border-policy changes).

    • Method × Outcome gaps:

  • Prior-immigrant wage effects (the largest estimated effect, ≈ −6%) rest on a single structural study — no quasi-experimental replication.
  • Skill-downgrading/returns evidence exists only for Germany ("other" method) — no causal design.

    • Data-infrastructure gap:

  • No linked dataset here pairs local immigration intensity with skill-disaggregated native wages and native out-migration — precisely the combination needed to reconcile the local-vs-national fault line.

    • Temporal gap:

  • Short-run/long-run distinction is asserted by structural models but not identified by any long-horizon panel design in the claim set.

    7. Suggested Research Directions

    1. DiD on a US immigration shock (closes the no-DiD-US gap).

    Approach: Difference-in-differences exploiting the geographic dispersion of a discrete shock (e.g., refugee resettlement quotas, H-1B cap changes, or DACA eligibility) across commuting zones.

    Identification: Treatment = pre-policy settlement networks predicting placement (shift-share/Bartik instrument); control = ex-ante similar zones with low exposure.

    Why: DiD on the US is entirely absent. It would directly test the short-run negative claims (−0.4% / −0.7%) at a credible causal standard the structural models cannot provide.

    2. Jointly estimate the spatial-attenuation parameter (resolves Fault Line 1).

    Approach: Panel model with immigration intensity, native out-migration, and skill-cell wages estimated simultaneously.

    Identification: Instrument immigration with origin-push × historical-network shift-share; instrument native mobility with housing-supply elasticity.

    Why: The 40–60% attenuation finding is the single biggest reason local and national estimates diverge. Recovering it structurally would reconcile Card vs. Borjas rather than re-litigate them.

    3. Prior-immigrant wage incidence, quasi-experimentally (replicates the −6% claim).

    Approach: Event-study on existing-immigrant cohorts following a new immigration wave to their metro area.

    Identification: Stacked event-study around enclave-targeted inflows.

    Why: The largest estimated effect in the entire literature rests on one structural paper. If real, it relocates the policy debate from "natives vs. immigrants" to "incumbent vs. new immigrants."

    4. OLS/DiD skill-downgrading study outside Germany (closes geography gap).

    Approach: Replicate the refugee-vs-economic-migrant skill-return gap in Canada (rich admin/landing data) or the US.

    Identification: Compare returns to identical measured skills across admission categories using linked employer-employee data.

    Why: The downgrading channel may explain why native effects are small (immigrants absorb the loss) — currently a single-country, single-method result.

    5. Build the missing linked dataset, then run a horse race.

    Approach: Construct a public panel pairing CZ-level immigration intensity, skill-disaggregated native AND immigrant-vintage wages, and net native migration.

    Why: Every QQA alignment above failed for lack of data. The highest-leverage contribution may be infrastructural, enabling all of the above.

    8. One-Paragraph Research Memo

    The literature has effectively settled that immigration's average effect on native wages is small — near zero or slightly positive in the long run, modestly negative in the short run — with the convincing structural evidence (+0.6% long-run, −0.4% short-run) and supportive US macro data (real median wages near series highs, sub-3.5% unemployment among less-than-HS workers, rapidly rising low-wage earnings) both pointing away from broad wage suppression. The live disputes are distributional and methodological: whether low-skilled and incumbent-immigrant workers bear concentrated losses (the −6% prior-immigrant estimate and the −0.7% no-HS estimate rest on thin, single-source structural foundations), and whether local-area designs systematically understate effects because native out-migration dilutes the shock by 40–60%. The decisive empirical gap is the complete absence of difference-in-differences designs for the US and Canada and the lack of any dataset linking local immigration intensity to skill- and vintage-disaggregated wages plus native mobility — none of the 19 claims could be tested against the available data. The most promising next study is a US difference-in-differences exploiting a discrete, geographically dispersed immigration shock (refugee quotas or visa-cap changes) with a shift-share instrument, designed to jointly recover the native out-migration attenuation parameter — this single design would simultaneously close the most glaring method gap and adjudicate the local-vs-national identification dispute that currently drives the field's disagreement.
