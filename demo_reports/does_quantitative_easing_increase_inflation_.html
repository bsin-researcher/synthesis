1. Research Question

Does quantitative easing (QE) — large-scale central bank asset purchases that expand the monetary base and central bank balance sheet — cause a measurable increase in inflation or inflation expectations? The question concerns a causal, directional effect of QE on the price level (and its expectational anchor), not merely correlation during crisis periods.

2. Literature Consensus

The pooled meta-analytic estimate is POSITIVE (+0.74 weighted), driven by 4 high-confidence claims using the strongest identification strategies: natural experiments, difference-in-differences (DiD), and structural DSGE models. The dominant view is that QE transmits to inflation and the real economy through identifiable channels rather than through a crude money-supply mechanism:

  • The refinancing/credit channel (DiD, US): QE1 MBS purchases lowered conforming mortgage rates by ~40bp extra, raised refinancing by +56%, and lifted durable consumption by +12% — a strong, well-identified transmission path to real demand.
  • The inflation-expectations channel (natural experiment, US): QE1 and QE2 are claimed to raise TIPS-implied (breakeven) inflation and lower real yields.
  • Structural DSGE (US): A financial-frictions model ties inflation to activity and monetary policy, predicting (notably) a modest decline in inflation after 2008 stress — a nuance that complicates the headline "QE raises inflation" reading.
  • Attention threshold (structural, US): Above ~4% inflation, public attention doubles and supply shocks become roughly twice as inflationary, explaining ~50% of the 2021 surge — relevant for why QE-era inflation eventually materialized.

    • The consensus is best stated as: QE plausibly raises inflation and inflation expectations through credit and expectations channels, but the effect is conditional and channel-dependent, not mechanical.

    3. Where the Literature Disagrees

    The debate is genuine and the fault lines are methodological:

  • Magnitude on the real economy is contested. One strand finds QE has a "low effect on economic growth" (liquidity traps, securitization frictions, distributional capture by banks). Another finds QE "was not successful in spurring bank lending" in the US. These directly undercut the demand-side path to inflation.
  • Macro effects are labeled "uncertain." The ECB-focused work finds QE reshapes bank-firm lending and intra-financial exposures but with ambiguous effects on output and prices — an explicit refusal to commit to the positive consensus.
  • Expectations may be inert. Survey-based work finds household and firm inflation expectations respond little to monetary policy announcements in low-inflation regimes, and New Zealand firm managers show poor anchoring after 25 years of inflation targeting. This contradicts the natural-experiment claim that QE moves breakeven inflation.
  • Methodological fault line: DiD and natural-experiment studies (micro, channel-specific) find effects; survey and some structural/"other" studies (macro, expectations) find weak or null effects. The disagreement is partly about what is measured — local channel effects versus aggregate price-level outcomes.

    • 4. What the Data Shows

    The FRED series describe the QE era directly:

  • WALCL (Fed balance sheet): Expanded from ~$713bn (2002) to a peak of ~$8.97tn, now ~$6.71tn — a roughly 12× expansion. This is the QE "treatment" variable.
  • BOGMBASE (monetary base): Rose from ~$577bn to a peak ~$6.41tn (now ~$5.47tn) — confirming massive base-money injection.
  • CPIAUCSL: Rose from 169.3 to 332.4 — but this is a secular trend across 25 years, not a step coincident with QE rounds. The sharp acceleration came in 2021–2022, more than a decade after QE1/QE2.
  • T5YIE (5-yr breakeven): Ranges −2.24% to 3.59%, latest 2.44% — i.e., inflation expectations remained roughly anchored near 2% across the entire QE period, including the large balance-sheet expansions of 2008–2014.

    • The key empirical test: regressing annual ΔWALCL on ΔT5YIE yields a near-zero, statistically insignificant slope (≈0.0000, Spearman r = −0.061, p = 0.564, n = 23). The data show no detectable QE-driven rise in inflation expectations. This directly challenges the natural-experiment claim that QE raised breakeven inflation. The narrative that base money expansion mechanically drove CPI is not supported: the largest balance-sheet expansion (2008–2014) coincided with below-target inflation and flat expectations; the 2021 inflation surge aligns better with supply shocks and the attention-threshold mechanism than with QE timing.

    Blunt conclusion: The literature leans positive, but the one claim the data can actually test — QE raising inflation expectations — is contradicted by the FRED series. Every other claim sits in an evidentiary blind spot because the required outcome variables (lending, GDP, refinancing, firm prices, attention, regime dummies) are absent from the available data. The +0.74 consensus is therefore not corroborated by the empirical layer; it is largely untested by it.

    6. Research Gaps (The Gap Matrix)

  • Structural × everywhere-but-US: No structural study exists for the euro area, Germany, Italy, New Zealand, global, or the multi-country (US/UK/euro/Japan) set. Structural modeling has been almost exclusively US-centric.
  • Outcome-variable gap: The empirical infrastructure lacks bank lending, real GDP, mortgage refinancing, firm-level prices/debt, and any inflation-attention proxy. Causal channel claims cannot be confirmed without these.
  • Regime-identification gap: No inflation-targeting regime indicators exist in the data, leaving the inflation-targeting "no-effect" claims wholly untestable.
  • Time-period gap: The post-2021 high-inflation regime (where the attention-threshold mechanism activates) is under-modeled structurally relative to the 2008–2014 low-inflation QE episodes.
  • Aggregate-vs-channel reconciliation gap: No study jointly identifies micro-channel effects (DiD) and aggregate price-level outcomes to explain why strong channel effects fail to move breakevens.

    • 7. Suggested Research Directions

    1. Cross-country structural QE model (euro area + Germany + Italy + Japan). Approach: estimate a multi-country DSGE/BVAR with QE shocks identified from announcement-date balance-sheet jumps. Identification: high-frequency event-study around purchase announcements. Why: fills the entire non-US structural gap and tests whether the US channel results generalize.

    2. Reconcile the channel/aggregate puzzle. Approach: link QE1 DiD refinancing effects to aggregate CPI via a quantified consumption-to-inflation pass-through. Identification: regional/lender exposure to MBS purchases as instrument, mapped to local CPI. Why: explains why strong micro effects (+12% durable consumption) coexist with flat breakevens (the contradiction in §5).

    3. Attention-threshold test with direct data. Approach: construct a Google/Twitter/media inflation-attention index and interact it with QE-era supply shocks. Identification: regime-switching threshold regression at ~4% CPI. Why: the attention mechanism is the most credible explanation for why QE-era inflation was dormant 2008–2019 then surged in 2021 — but it is currently untested empirically.

    4. QE → bank lending → prices, with lending data. Approach: panel of bank balance sheets pre/post QE rounds. Identification: bank-level reserve injection heterogeneity (DiD). Why: directly adjudicates the contested "QE failed to spur lending" claim, the weakest link in the inflation transmission chain.

    5. Expectations-anchoring under QE. Approach: combine T5YIE with household/firm survey expectations across QE rounds. Identification: high-frequency announcement windows. Why: tests whether the null breakeven response (§4) reflects genuine anchoring or measurement, reconciling the survey "no-effect" strand with the natural-experiment "positive" strand.

    8. One-Paragraph Research Memo

    The literature tilts toward QE being inflationary (pooled +0.74, anchored by high-confidence DiD and natural-experiment evidence of strong refinancing, consumption, and expectations channels), but this consensus rests on US micro-channel studies and is essentially untested at the aggregate price level. The single claim our empirical data can directly evaluate — that QE raised breakeven inflation expectations — is contradicted: the relationship between annual Fed balance-sheet changes and 5-year breakevens is statistically zero (p=0.564, n=23), and inflation expectations stayed anchored near 2% even through the largest 2008–2014 balance-sheet expansion. The central empirical gap is the absence of outcome data (bank lending, real GDP, refinancing, firm prices, and any inflation-attention proxy) needed to confirm the proposed transmission channels, compounded by a near-total lack of structural studies outside the US. The most valuable next study would reconcile the channel/aggregate puzzle — using lender- or region-level QE exposure as an instrument to trace micro refinancing effects through to aggregate CPI — while jointly testing the ~4% attention threshold that best explains why QE coincided with dormant inflation for a decade before the 2021 surge. Until that reconciliation exists, the honest verdict is that QE's effect on inflation (as opposed to asset prices and credit conditions) remains unconfirmed and, on the only directly testable margin here, unsupported.
