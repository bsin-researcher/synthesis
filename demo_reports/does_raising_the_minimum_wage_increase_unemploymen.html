1. Research Question

Does raising the statutory minimum wage cause an increase in unemployment? Specifically, do binding minimum wage increases reduce employment levels, slow employment growth, or raise the unemployment rate — and if so, for whom and over what horizon?

2. Literature Consensus

There is no clean consensus. The pooled meta-analytic estimate is MIXED (weighted score -0.18, 11 claims, 4 high-confidence), with a near-even split: 3 positive, 3 negative, 3 no-effect, 2 ambiguous. The slightly negative weighted score reflects a marginal lean toward "minimal-to-negative employment effects," not a dominant disemployment finding.

The strongest empirically-anchored finding in this corpus is disaggregation-dependent:

  • Aggregate level effects are small or null. The Card-Krueger-style natural experiment (NJ/PA fast food) and the 138-state DiD analysis (no_effect, SUPPORTED) both find essentially no change in low-wage employment levels. The German 22% hike (DiD) found no employment-retention effect, only reduced hours among minijobbers.
  • Distributional/incidence effects dominate. The Hungarian natural experiment and "Who Pays for the Minimum Wage?" both find small negative elasticities with ~75% of costs passed to consumers and the remainder to firms — labor demand absorbs the shock through prices and capital substitution, not mass layoffs.
  • Demographic and dynamic effects are where positive unemployment effects appear. Mincer's theoretical work (positive, STRONGLY_SUPPORTED) and the job-growth-dynamics study (negative for growth, STRONGLY_SUPPORTED) both find that effects show up in margins other than the aggregate level: teen unemployment via participation responses, and slowed multi-year job growth rather than an immediate level drop.

    • Methodologically, the highest-weight evidence comes from natural experiments and difference-in-differences, with structural and theoretical work supplying the mechanisms.

    3. Where the Literature Disagrees

    The genuine fault lines:

    1. Level vs. growth vs. composition. Studies measuring employment levels (Card-Krueger, 138-state DiD) find null effects. Studies measuring employment dynamics/growth find negative effects. This is partly a measurement-window disagreement, not a contradiction — the job-growth study explicitly argues the damage is in slowed hiring over years, invisible to short-window level comparisons.

    2. Aggregate vs. demographic. Mincer's positive unemployment finding for teenagers coexists with aggregate null findings because participation responses and group-specific elasticities differ. The literature disagrees on whether "unemployment" should be measured economy-wide or for binding sub-populations.

    3. Institutional regime dependence. The Western Europe claim attributes the 3%→11% unemployment rise to wage-setting institutions and reservation wages — a structural/macro view at odds with the micro natural-experiment tradition that finds small effects. These are arguably studying different objects (rigid continental labor markets vs. flexible US low-wage sectors).

    4. The explicitly contested case: Bulgarian data is flagged as actively contested, examining correlation direction "against a backdrop of conflicting literature" — i.e., the underlying disagreement is acknowledged within the primary source itself.

    The methodological tension: natural experiments/DiD (clean identification, narrow scope, short horizon) vs. structural/macro (broad scope, longer horizon, weaker causal identification). The two traditions reach different conclusions partly because they answer different questions.

    4. What the Data Shows

    The FRED/World Bank series describe the US (and global) macro environment, not causal effects:

  • Federal minimum wage (STTMINWGFG): flat at $7.25 since 2009 (range $5.15–$7.25). This is the single most important data fact: the federal floor has been nominally frozen for ~15 years and has fallen sharply in real terms. Any US disemployment effect from the federal minimum is therefore unlikely in recent data — the binding action is at the state level (138 state changes referenced in the literature).
  • Unemployment (UNRATE): 4.3% latest, range 3.4–14.8%. The high end is the 2020 COVID spike — unrelated to minimum wage. The structural low-4% range during a period of frozen federal minimum is consistent with the "no aggregate disemployment" camp.
  • Nonfarm payrolls (PAYEMS): at series high (159,001K), monotonic growth. Employment grew throughout, again inconsistent with a strong aggregate disemployment story at the federal level.
  • Teen unemployment (LNS14000012): 12.3% latest, range 9.4–32.8%. Teen unemployment is structurally ~3x the headline rate. This is consistent with — but does not prove — Mincer's claim that low-wage demographics bear the unemployment margin.
  • Food services employment (CES7072200001): near series high (12,265K of 12,285K max). The sector most exposed to minimum wages shows robust employment — consistent with Card-Krueger.
  • Average weekly hours (CES0500000007): 33.7, range 33.0–34.4, drifting down. Mildly consistent with the German finding that adjustment occurs on the hours margin rather than the headcount margin.

    • Bottom line: US macro data is consistent with the "small/null aggregate level effect" view, but the data cannot identify causal effects — the federal minimum has been static, so there is no national treatment to exploit. The data neither confirms nor refutes the growth-dynamics or demographic claims because those require sub-national, sub-population identification the aggregate series lack.

    6. Research Gaps (The Gap Matrix)

  • Geography × method gaps: Every listed geography lacks a meta-analysis (Hungary, Western Europe, Germany, Bulgaria, US, global, NJ/PA, Spain). The literature is a collection of single-country point estimates with no formal cross-country quantitative synthesis.
  • Time-horizon gap: The level-vs-growth disagreement is fundamentally about horizon. Almost no study cleanly separates the 0–2 year null level effect from the 3–5 year growth deceleration using the same data and design.
  • Demographic resolution gap: Mincer's participation-channel mechanism is supported but under-tested with modern microdata; the teen unemployment series exists but has not been causally linked.
  • Hours-margin gap: The German "adjust on hours, not headcount" finding (and the declining US weekly-hours series) is essentially untested in the US natural-experiment literature.
  • Real vs. nominal gap: With the federal minimum frozen since 2009, the entire recent US identification rests on state variation — under-exploited as a panel.

    • 7. Suggested Research Directions

    1. Multi-horizon state-panel DiD on US data (2009–2026).

    Approach: Exploit the frozen federal floor as a clean baseline; treat each state minimum-wage increase as a staggered treatment using modern staggered-DiD estimators (Callaway-Sant'Anna). Why: Directly resolves the level-vs-growth disagreement by estimating dynamic event-study coefficients at 1, 3, and 5 years on the same sample — the single most important unresolved tension in this corpus.

    2. Hours-vs-headcount decomposition.

    Approach: Pair CES employment with CES average-weekly-hours at state-sector level across state minimum-wage changes. Why: Tests the German finding domestically; the declining US hours series suggests the adjustment margin hypothesis is live and untested in US natural experiments.

    3. Demographic participation-margin study.

    Approach: Triple-difference on teen (LNS14000012) vs. adult unemployment across high- vs. low-bite states. Why: Mincer's positive-unemployment claim is supported in aggregate but its participation mechanism has never been isolated — this would convert a theoretical claim into an identified one.

    4. First formal cross-country meta-analysis.

    Approach: Harmonize elasticity estimates from Germany, Hungary, Spain, US, and Western Europe into a random-effects meta-regression with institutional-regime moderators (collective bargaining coverage, reservation-wage generosity). Why: No meta-analysis exists for any geography; this would test whether the "regime dependence" hypothesis explains the positive/null disagreement.

    5. Bulgaria/Eastern Europe natural experiment.

    Approach: Resolve the explicitly contested Bulgarian case with a regression-discontinuity or DiD design using EU-wide administrative data. Why: It is the one claim flagged as actively contested, and Eastern Europe (high minimum-wage bite relative to median) is a high-leverage testing ground.

    8. One-Paragraph Research Memo

    The minimum-wage employment literature is genuinely mixed (pooled score -0.18), but the disagreement is largely artifactual: studies measure different margins. Aggregate employment levels show null effects (Card-Krueger, 138-state DiD; supported by US data showing record payrolls and ~4% unemployment despite a federal minimum frozen at $7.25 since 2009), while employment growth slows over 3–5 years (strongly supported, p=0.036) and demographic unemployment rises for low-wage groups like teenagers (Mincer; strongly supported, p=0.032). The critical empirical gap is that 8 of 11 literature claims span geographies (Germany, Hungary, Spain, Bulgaria, Western Europe) the available US-centric data cannot test, and no formal meta-analysis exists for any region. The most promising next study is a staggered-DiD state panel (2009–2026) with dynamic event-study coefficients at 1/3/5 years, which uses the frozen federal floor as a clean baseline and would directly reconcile the level-versus-growth contradiction that currently masquerades as scholarly disagreement. A second-priority companion study should decompose the headcount-versus-hours adjustment margin, since both the German evidence and the declining US weekly-hours series suggest firms absorb minimum-wage shocks through hours and prices rather than layoffs.
